# The escalation curve, under two budgets

**Escalating one field to `gen-8b` breaks the declared latency ceiling.** Not
"costs too much" — breaks it. And every cheaper escalation target that fits under
the ceiling completes zero whole documents, including with perfect foresight.

Measured 21 August 2026 from stored rows. No GPU.

## The budget, declared before the measurement

    corpus budget      10 % of fields
    per-document cap    2 escalated fields
    latency ceiling  2,000 ms per document

Written before the results, because a curve with several points offers several
chances to clear a bar where a single recommendation offered one. The condition
is read at the declared budget, never at the most flattering point.

## Why one budget was not enough

A corpus budget bounds cost. It does not bound latency, because **escalations
arrive in clusters**: a hard document is hard on several fields at once, and
ranking by signal count makes the clustering worse rather than diluting it.

At the "at least one signal" step, **all thirty documents escalate at least two
fields and fifteen escalate four or five.** A 68 % corpus budget is not 68 %
spread thinly; it is nearly every field of every document.

That is why the previous version of this table was misleading. It reported mean
latency per document — 1,149 ms against a 2,000 ms ceiling, comfortable — and the
mean hid that every escalated document was over.

## The curve has two steps, not a continuum

| step | fields | share of corpus |
|---|---|---|
| score ≥ 2 | 4 | 2.7 % |
| score ≥ 1 | 103 | 68.7 % |

There is nothing between. A client asking for 5 % gets 2.7 %; a client asking for
20 % gets 2.7 % as well. Effective rates are reported beside requested ones.

## Correction: one escalation is admissible, and it gains 26 fields

An earlier version of this report said escalating one field to `gen-8b` breaks
the ceiling "for a single field on any document". That is wrong, and emitting the
figure per field is what showed it.

| escalated field | ms/document | against the 2,000 ms ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| `name` | 2,069 | over by 69 |
| `birth` | 2,335 | over by 335 |
| `document` | 2,218 | over by 218 |
| **`country`** | **1,863** | **under by 137** |
| `address` | 2,326 | over by 326 |

Four of five, not five of five. **`country` fits**, because the recommended
routing sends it to `rules`, whose latency is nil, so replacing it costs only
`gen-8b`'s own time.

And that one admissible escalation is worth having:

|  | fields | whole docs | €/1000 | max ms |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| no escalation | 44/150 | 0/30 | 1.91 | 968 |
| `country` → `gen-8b` when suspect | **70/150** | 0/30 | 2.21 | 1,863 |

Twenty-six fields gained, **0–26 paired**, p = 3×10⁻⁸, decided. Wilson 29.3 %
[22.6–37.1] to 46.7 % [38.9–54.6], intervals that do not touch. Thirty
escalations, 0.30 € per thousand more, and it stays under the ceiling.

Not a single extra whole document, because the other four fields keep failing.
So the earlier conclusion holds where it was about documents and was wrong where
it was about escalation as such: **escalation buys fields, at a price, within the
budget — and it does not buy files.**

## The arithmetic that settles it

Base routing: 968 ms per document. Ceiling: 2,000 ms. One escalated field costs:

| target | document total | |
|---|---|---|
| `small` | 986 ms | fits |
| `gen-0.6b` | 1,190 ms | fits |
| `gen-4b` | 1,677 ms | fits |
| **`gen-8b`** | **2,069 ms** | **over** |

So escalation to the only tier accurate enough to matter is **inadmissible under
the declared latency ceiling**, for a single field, on any document. And
escalation to the tiers that do fit returns zero whole documents at every budget,
with the oracle, on the previous measurement.

That is a cleaner result than "escalation does not pay". The escalation that fits
the budget returns nothing; the escalation that could return something is not
allowed to run.

## Withdrawn: the "best fixed routing at half the price"

An earlier version of this report said the best fixed routing inside the cascade's
budget completed two documents at 0.99 € per thousand. **That routing runs at
3,273 ms per document against a 2,000 ms ceiling. It is not cheaper; it is
forbidden** — and it appeared in no file, only in this prose.

The search that produced it bounded cost and never latency, inside the report
whose finding is that escalation fails on that same ceiling. Withdrawn, and the
search now applies both budgets. Every routing is emitted to `escalade.json` with
its admissibility.

## The routing changes completely with the corpus

Both budgets applied — 2,000 ms per document, 40 € per thousand — over all 7,776
routings, of which 4,321 are admissible. Accuracy from the hard-corpus rows, price
and latency from the published relevé.

| | routing | fields | whole docs | €/1000 | ms/doc |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| recommended, tuned on clean | large, rules, rules, rules, gen-4b | **44/150** | 0/30 | 1.84 | 968 |
| best admissible, by fields | gen-0.6b, gen-0.6b, rules, gen-8b, gen-0.6b | **83/150** | 0/30 | **0.59** | 1,631 |
| best admissible, by documents | large, gen-0.6b, small, gen-8b, gen-0.6b | 77/150 | 1/30 | 2.32 | 1,475 |

**Nearly twice the fields, at a third of the price.** Paired over the same 150
fields: 51–12 for the alternative, p = 7×10⁻⁷, decided. Wilson: 29.3 %
[22.6–37.1] against 55.3 % [47.3–63.1], intervals that do not touch.

Both counts are above this repository's floor of twenty, so unlike the
whole-document column this comparison is quotable.

**Why the recommendation collapses.** It is cheap because three of five fields go
to `rules` for free. On broken documents `rules` returns a blank 133 times in 164,
so the three free fields become three empty ones, and the economics invert: the
alternative pays for generative tiers on four fields and still costs a third as
much, because it drops `large` — the most expensive tier in the price model, an
API at 1.60 per thousand against `gen-8b` at 0.37 of local machine time.

**The caveat that limits this.** The best admissible routing was chosen on the
same thirty documents that score it. Its figure is flattered, exactly as a prompt
tuned on the test set is. What the comparison establishes is **the fragility of
the recommended routing**, not the value of this particular alternative — for that
a second half of hard corpus would be needed, and none exists.

## Length does not confound the corpus comparison

Asked whether document length, rather than cleanliness, drives the difference
between corpora — because prefill dominates generative cost and cost ratios
between tiers move with input length.

It does not confound it here, and it runs the other way: the hard documents
average **128 characters against the clean corpus's 166**, a factor of 0.77. They
are shorter, so cheaper to prefill. The accuracy collapse on them is therefore not
a length effect being mistaken for a cleanliness effect — if anything the shorter
documents make it starker.

Prefill share measured on this machine at these sizes: 34 ms of ~200 for
`gen-0.6b`, 134 of ~640 for `gen-4b`, 235 of ~900 for `gen-8b` — 17 to 26 %, not
the 90 % that holds at a thousand input tokens. Both figures are consistent; they
describe different document sizes, and ours are small.

## Latencies barely differ between the two corpora

Asked whether the ceiling verdict is a corpus effect: no. Median per-field
latency, hard against clean: `small` ×1.27, `large` ×1.02, `gen-0.6b` ×0.95,
`gen-4b` ×0.85, `gen-8b` ×0.82. The generative tiers are slightly *faster* on
these documents. The ceiling arithmetic holds on either corpus.

## The reservation that governs the counts

**Zero, one and two whole documents out of thirty**, against a floor of twenty
below which this repository publishes no rate. Nothing in the whole-document
column is distinguishable from anything else in it.

The latency finding does not depend on those counts. It is arithmetic over
measured per-field latencies, and it holds whatever the accuracy turns out to be.
